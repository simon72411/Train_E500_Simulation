Braking model

Distance = (V₁² − V₂²) ÷ (D + gradient‰/4) ＋ V₁ Application D Pure braking rate ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── Station stop 50→0 11 1.50 High-speed deceleration 60~95 8 1.11 The final term V₁ is the dead-travel distance: V km/h = V/3.6 m/s, and × 3.6 s = V metres (an exact identity).
Both expressions share the same structure and differ only in the divisor. The separation reflects two established characteristics of tread braking: the friction coefficient rises as speed falls, and it declines as accumulated friction work heats the block. Both are addressed below with reference to the international literature.

Speed dependence of tread-brake friction

For tread brakes with cast-iron blocks, the friction coefficient is speed dependent, and European longitudinal-dynamics models compute it from Karwatzki's empirical expression, “which predicts larger values of the friction coefficient at lower speed”. “for disc-braked vehicles the coefficient is by contrast approximately constant” — a distinction directly relevant to this consist, in which eight coaches carry composition blocks and two carry cast iron.

Full-scale stop-braking experiments on Indian Railways brake blocks found that “the friction coefficient decreased with increasing brake pressure and sliding velocity until it reached a critical value”, and that “apparent friction coefficient of brake blocks was seen to depend on brake load, sliding speed, as well as sliding distance”. A review of railway braking systems notes that “the coefficient of friction among the wheel tread and cast-iron brake shoe strongly depend on contact pressure, applied force on each shoe and instantaneous running speed of the vehicle”, and that “in the practical calculation, various empirical relations are obtained by the experiments”.

The formulas above are derived from cast-iron block tests. Pin-on-disc experiments comparing three commercial block materials found that “cast iron showed the highest friction coefficient, particle emission and wear loss and organic composite exhibited the lowest”; wheel-steel shoe-braking tests likewise report that “organic composite brake samples outperform those in cast iron… stable friction coefficients”. Composite blocks developed for locomotive use are described as having “a reduced coefficient of friction… high wear resistance and more stable frictional properties at high speeds”.

A consist that is predominantly composition-blocked should therefore exhibit a smaller low-speed gain than the cast-iron formulas predict. The measured ratio of 1.304 against a predicted 1.374 is consistent with that expectation.

Thermal fade

Friction work heats both block and wheel, reducing the coefficient as an application continues. Models that resolve this explicitly include Gralla's, which “consists of four terms A, P, V and W, depending on initial and instantaneous velocity, friction work and brake type” — the friction-work term being the fade mechanism. Pin-on-disc work at elevated temperatures found that “the generation of oxide layers on the worn cast iron surface resulted in a decrease in friction”. A study of composite blocks under continuous braking on long steep gradients reports that “when the speed increases to 100 km/h, the friction coefficient of the braking interface deteriorates severely… seriously endangering braking safety”.

Numerical integration: equations and implementation

Coasting profiles are computed by step-by-step numerical integration. There are only two update equations; everything else is table lookup, since gradient and curvature vary with position.

Equation of motion

Net force f = tractive − r(V) − gradient − 600/R − braking [kgf/tonf] Acceleration a = f × K ÷ (1+λ) [km/h/s] K = 0.0353, 1+λ = 1.08

Discretisation (forward Euler)

Time is divided into steps of length dt; each step updates both speed and position. Position must be carried because gradient and curvature are functions of position.

v ← v + a·dt speed update [km/h] chainage ← chainage − (v/3.6)·dt position update [m]

Implementation

dt = 0.005 # 5 ms step v = 93.0 # initial speed, km/h km = 12900.0 # initial chainage, m t = 0.0 while km > 12100: g = grade(km) # mass-weighted effective gradient (lookup) if t >= 3.0: # brake acts only after 3 s dead travel a = -1.11 - g*K/(1+λ) # pure braking rate 1.11, r(V) NOT added else: a = -g*K/(1+λ) # during dead travel, gradient only v += a * dt km -= v/3.6 * dt t += dt
The braking rate must be the resistance-free value. The mental divisors (9 for stopping, 8 for deceleration) already absorb r(V) from their calibration; reusing that equivalent rate inside an integration while also adding r(V) double-counts resistance. This caused the earlier Nan'ao (27 vs 48 km/h) and Su'ao New discrepancies.

Relationship to the energy method

Integration Δv = a·dt, repeated thousands of times Energy form v² = v₀² − d·D (D = 7.2 × deceleration rate)

When a is constant the two are mathematically equivalent — the energy form is the closed-form solution of the same differential equation. Integration earns its keep when a varies with position: the gradient changes every metre, and the energy form can only use an average.

Division of labour in this model: integration for coasting profiles (large gradient variation, no large speed reduction); energy method for braking distances (calibrated, paired parameters, avoiding mix-up errors).

Statistical validation

Of 35 validation cases, 23 are independent (parameters fixed, data not used in fitting). However these 23 span six categories measuring six different physical quantities; pooling them would violate the identical-distribution assumption. Formal tests are therefore applied only to the coasting group (n=14, all in km/h); the remaining groups have n≤5 and are reported descriptively only.

The mean allows opposite signs to cancel; RMS squares first and does not. Here μ is small while RMS is larger, which is exactly the signature of no systematic bias but real per-case scatter.

No formal test is applied to the braking group (RMS 10.3%, n=5). Its mean error of −7.1% is clearly larger in magnitude than other categories, but n=5 cannot pass any test. The physical mechanism is nevertheless known: composition shoes fade thermally over long high-speed applications (1.29 measured over 546 m, 0.98 over 1200 m), while the model uses a single divisor. The physical mechanism is therefore given weight despite the absence of statistical significance — and this is precisely why practice retains the Car-5 checkpoint and additional reduction.

Measurement uncertainty is about ±1.5–2 km/h (speedometer read to whole numbers ±0.5; positions stated as "approximately" ±50–100 m).
Below 1 km the model error is under the noise floor and cannot be resolved; above 1 km there is a discernible error, but it remains within operational tolerance.
Not independently validated: rotational inertia factor 1.08 (the operating-theory manual gives 1.06, POLIMI measured 1.039; three back-calculations gave 0.82 / 1.26 / 1.35 — too scattered to determine), and the shape of the braking curve (three different shapes reproduce the same stopping distances; the data cannot distinguish them).

The two methods must be paired correctly and never mixed: the mental divisors (9 for stopping, 8 for deceleration) already absorb running resistance from their calibration; step-by-step integration must instead add r(V) explicitly and use a resistance-free pure braking rate. Applying the mental equivalent rate inside an integration while also adding r(V) double-counts resistance — this was the actual cause of the earlier Nan'ao (27 vs 48 km/h) and Su'ao New discrepancies, not the numerical method (halving dt from 0.05 to 0.0005 changes the result by less than 0.03 km/h).
Speed range also matters: pure braking is about 1.49 when stopping and about 1.11 at high speed — a 34% difference.

Empirically adequate ≠ physically identified. The model reproduces observations and predicts unseen data, but several parameters compensate for one another; their individual values are not necessarily correct.
